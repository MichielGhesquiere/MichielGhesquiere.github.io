Multimodal Retinal Imaging for the Detection of Early Alzheimer's Disease

Alzheimer's disease starts silently, decades before memory begins to fail. We asked whether a set of eye scans could pick up signs linked to amyloid build-up in the brain in people who are still cognitively normal or only mildly impaired, and whether combining different kinds of scan works better than any one alone.

The problem

Finding Alzheimer's early is hard, and it matters

In Alzheimer's disease, two proteins, amyloid-β and tau, slowly accumulate in the brain long before symptoms appear. Worldwide, an estimated 315 million people are thought to be in this silent "preclinical" phase: amyloid-positive, but cognitively normal.

Spotting the disease at this stage is becoming more important. New antibody treatments that target amyloid can moderately slow cognitive decline, clinical trials need to recruit people early, and lifestyle changes have the most room to help. But the reference tests, a PET scan of the brain or a spinal tap to analyse cerebrospinal fluid, are expensive or invasive, and not suited to screening large groups of people. Blood tests are a promising alternative. So is the eye.

Why the retina?

The retina grows out of the same embryonic tissue as the brain and is directly connected to it through the optic nerve. It is the only part of the central nervous system we can look at directly, with light, in a few seconds, and without any injection. And the cameras to do it are already in eye clinics. If Alzheimer's-related changes show up in the retina, an eye exam could one day help decide who should get a PET scan or be invited to a trial.

The approach

Three cameras, three different views of the same retina

Each imaging technique captures a different aspect of the retina. The underlying idea: Alzheimer's is not one process but several (protein build-up, loss of nerve cells, blood vessel problems), so a combination of views might see more than any single one.

HSI

Hyperspectral imaging

A camera that photographs the back of the eye in many narrow colour bands at once, from blue to orange (≈460–620 nm), in a 0.2 ms snapshot. How tissue reflects and scatters each colour depends on its microscopic make-up.

We measured: the texture of the macula in each colour band
OCT

Optical coherence tomography

An "ultrasound with light" that produces cross-sections of the retina, resolving its individual layers at micrometre scale.

We measured: thickness of the ganglion cell–inner plexiform layer (GC-IPL), where the retina's output neurons and their connections live. Thinning points to neurodegeneration.
CFP

Colour fundus photography

The standard colour photo of the back of the eye. A deep learning model traced every artery and vein automatically.

We measured: 24 vascular biomarkers, such as the average width of arterioles (CRAE) and venules (CRVE)

Who took part

The 40 participants (aged 50–85) came from RetAD, a longitudinal study at UZ Leuven. Crucially, everyone had an amyloid PET scan, so we knew who had amyloid in their brain, rather than relying on symptoms alone.

For each modality we trained a simple, transparent model (logistic regression) to tell Aβ+ from Aβ− participants, and tested it with leave-one-out cross-validation: the model is trained on 39 people and asked to predict the 40th, repeated for every person. That way, nobody is ever scored by a model that has seen them.

A closer look

Reading texture, not just colour

Raw brightness in a hyperspectral image varies a lot from person to person: lens clarity, pupil size and camera flash all play a role. In our data, the normalised brightness per colour band overlapped heavily between the two groups. So instead of asking how bright the retina is, we asked how the brightness is arranged.

One classic texture measure is GLCM correlation (from the gray-level co-occurrence matrix). It asks: if I know the brightness of one pixel, how well can I predict the brightness of its neighbour? A smooth, uniform texture gives a value close to 1. A patchier, more heterogeneous texture gives a lower value. Try it:

In real retinas, all values sat between roughly 0.90 and 0.96, and the difference between the group medians was only about 0.01: invisible to the eye, but measurable.

What we found

Five findings, and how sure we are about each

1

In blue light, amyloid-positive retinas look slightly more heterogeneous

Across all colour bands and regions, the strongest candidate signal was GLCM correlation at 466 nm, the shortest (bluest) wavelength we analysed. Amyloid-positive participants had lower values, meaning a more heterogeneous local texture. The difference was clearest in the lower (inferior) half of the macula, where texture alone separated the groups with an AUC of 0.72.

Why blue? Shorter wavelengths scatter more strongly off very small structures in tissue. Earlier studies, in Alzheimer's mouse models and in patients, also reported the largest retinal optical changes at short wavelengths, below about 500–565 nm.

How sure are we?

This was an exploratory screen: we compared 14 wavelengths × 3 regions = 42 combinations. After correcting for that many comparisons, none of them remained statistically significant. And because 466 nm was picked using the whole dataset, the performance estimate is optimistic: when we let the model pick its wavelength inside each cross-validation fold, the full-macula AUC dropped from 0.69 to 0.52 (and in the inferior half only from 0.72 to 0.68). Encouragingly, 466 nm was still the wavelength chosen most often (in 97.5% of folds for the inferior half).

Also, texture is an optical measure, not a chemical one: these data don't tell us that we are literally seeing amyloid in the retina. Tau, inflammation, or other tissue changes could contribute as well.

We also checked how stable the measurement is. Repeating the scan with different flash settings on the same day gave very consistent values (ICC up to 0.88). Across visits months apart, repeatability was moderate (ICC 0.60 for the full macula).

2

The ganglion cell layer carries a structural signal, spread across the macula

On OCT, the overall pattern of GC-IPL thickness across the nine macular regions was associated with amyloid status (p = 0.009, adjusted for age and sex). The nerve fibre layer (RNFL) was not. Interestingly, no single region differed significantly on its own: the information lies in the spatial pattern, not in one spot.

A model using all nine GC-IPL regions reached an AUC of 0.73. And, like HSI, the inferior regions were far more informative than the superior ones (AUC 0.71 vs. 0.45). Two very different techniques pointing at the same part of the retina is intriguing. Since both were measured in the same people, though, it is a convergence worth following up, not an independent replication.

3

Large retinal vessels added little

Of 24 vascular measures, only arteriole width (slightly wider in Aβ+) and the number of venous endpoints showed nominal differences, and neither survived correction for multiple comparisons. Arteriole and venule width together gave an AUC of 0.68, and adding them to the best model did not help (0.84 → 0.81).

That doesn't mean blood vessels are irrelevant in Alzheimer's. Static vessel width on a photo is a coarse measure, and vessel width is also strongly affected by blood pressure, diabetes and heart disease. Changes at the level of the tiniest capillaries (measurable with OCT angiography) or in how vessels react (dynamic vessel analysis, now being collected in RetAD) may be more sensitive.

4

Combining texture and structure gave the best performance

The strongest model combined HSI texture with the nine OCT GC-IPL regions. How to read the numbers:

How sure are we?

Every model did better than chance (tested by shuffling the labels 1,000 times; p = 0.001 for HSI + OCT). But the confidence intervals are wide, as expected with 40 people. The gain of the combination over HSI alone (+0.15, 95% CI −0.01 to 0.30) or over OCT alone (+0.11, −0.04 to 0.25) was not statistically significant. The right reading is: the two techniques seem to carry complementary information, which is a hypothesis worth testing at scale, not yet proof that combining them is better.

5

Across disease stages, the texture signal dips and then rises again

To look beyond the early stage, we added 39 participants from our earlier study (22 cognitively normal, 17 with Alzheimer's dementia), imaged with the same camera. Texture was lower in the early amyloid-positive group than in controls, but in people with dementia it was back up, not different from controls.

A similar "strongest in the middle" pattern was reported in an earlier hyperspectral study, whose authors suggested that small, soluble amyloid clusters early in the disease may affect light differently from the larger, fibrillar aggregates found later. That could explain our pattern too, but it remains a hypothesis. It also has a practical implication: a marker that goes down and then back up cannot simply be read as "lower is worse".

How sure are we?

These are different people at different stages, not the same people followed over time, and the dementia group came from a separate cohort. Controls from both cohorts had similar values (p = 0.503), which is somewhat reassuring, but stage and cohort remain partly entangled. Only longitudinal follow-up can show whether this is how the retina actually changes over the course of the disease.

The bigger picture

Why combining views makes biological sense

Researchers classify Alzheimer's biomarkers by the process they reflect: Amyloid, Tau, Neurodegeneration, and Vascular damage as a common co-pathology. Our three retinal views loosely line up with that framework.

This offers a plausible explanation for why HSI and OCT might complement each other: they may sample different disease processes. But it is a way of framing hypotheses. The retinal measures do not directly quantify the corresponding processes in the brain, and future studies need to test these links against established brain and blood biomarkers.

Reading this responsibly

What this study does not show

  • It is small. 40 participants, 17 amyloid-positive. That means wide confidence intervals and a real risk that the models are tuned to this particular group.
  • It has not been externally validated. The key HSI feature was discovered in the same data it was tested on, and no independent cohort with PET-confirmed amyloid status was available.
  • It doesn't test specificity. People with glaucoma or other neurological diseases were excluded, so we don't yet know whether these signals are specific to Alzheimer's.
  • It is not a diagnostic test. The results are hypothesis-generating estimates of what might be possible, not measures of clinical performance.

What it does provide: preliminary evidence that the retina carries measurable signals linked to brain amyloid, a concrete candidate feature (466 nm texture), a candidate region (the inferior macula), and a reason to pursue multimodal retinal imaging in larger, longitudinal cohorts.

If you read nothing else

Five take-aways

  1. The retina shows signals linked to brain amyloid, even before dementia.In preclinical and prodromal Alzheimer's, confirmed by PET.
  2. Texture beats brightness.How light is arranged across the retina was more informative than how much light is reflected, with blue light (466 nm) standing out.
  3. For OCT, the spatial pattern matters.No single region differed on its own, but the pattern of GC-IPL thickness across the macula did. Both HSI and OCT pointed to the lower half of the macula.
  4. HSI and OCT look complementary.Together they reached AUC 0.84, the best of all combinations, though not yet significantly better than either alone.
  5. Next: bigger, longer, prespecified.Larger longitudinal cohorts with the wavelength and region fixed in advance, plus capillary-level and functional vessel imaging, which is part of the ongoing RetAD study.
